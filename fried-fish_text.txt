This Island-Style Crispy Escovitch Fish is a classic Caribbean staple, perfect for your web design project. It features red snapper fried to a golden crunch and topped with a zesty, colorful medley of pickled vegetables.

Ingredients

The Fish
2 lbs Whole Red Snapper (cleaned, scaled, and scored)
2 tsp Salt
1 tsp Black pepper
1 tbsp Garlic powder
1 cup Vegetable oil (for shallow frying)
2 slices Fresh lime (for cleaning the fish)

The Escovitch Sauce (Pickled Topping)
1 large Red onion (thinly sliced into rings)
1 large Carrot (julienned or thinly sliced)
1/2 Chayote or "Cho-cho" (julienned)
1-2 Scotch Bonnet peppers (seeded and sliced into strips)
1/2 cup White vinegar
1 tsp Pimento seeds (allspice berries)
1 tsp Granulated sugar
1/2 tsp Salt

Instructions
Prep the Fish: Rub the fish with lime juice and rinse with cold water. Pat completely dry. Season inside and out with salt, black pepper, and garlic powder.
Fry until Golden: Heat oil in a large skillet over medium-high heat. Fry the fish for about 5–7 minutes per side until the skin is crispy and the meat is opaque. Remove and drain on paper towels.
Prepare the Sauce: In a separate small pan, bring vinegar, pimento seeds, sugar, and salt to a simmer.
Sauté Vegetables: Add the onions, carrots, chayote, and scotch bonnet peppers to the vinegar. Sauté for 2–3 minutes—the vegetables should remain slightly crunchy.
Assemble: Arrange the fried fish on a platter and pour the hot vinegar and vegetable mixture over the top. Let it sit for at least 15 minutes before serving to allow the flavors to penetrate the fish.

Prep time: 20 minutes (includes cleaning the fish and slicing the vegetables)
Cook time: 15 minutes
Total time: 35 minutes (plus 15 minutes for flavors to soak)
Number of servings: 4 servings

## Origins & History
Escovitch fish is a beloved Jamaican classic with a rich history that dates back to the 16th century. The term is a local adaptation of the Spanish word escabeche, a technique for preserving fried meat or fish in an acidic marinade introduced to the island by Spanish and Portuguese Jews. Long before modern refrigeration, this pickling method was essential for keeping food fresh in the Caribbean heat. Over generations, Jamaica transformed this European method into a signature island dish by infusing it with bold, local ingredients like fiery Scotch bonnet peppers and aromatic pimento (allspice). [1, 2, 3, 4, 5, 6] 
## Traditions & Serving Suggestions
While it is eaten year-round at seaside restaurants, Escovitch fish holds deep cultural significance as a traditional meal for Good Friday. It is traditionally enjoyed with: [1, 7] 

* [Bammy]: A dense, fried cassava flatbread that perfectly soaks up the tangy vinegar sauce.
* Festival: Sweet, fried dough dumplings that provide a delicious contrast to the spicy, acidic fish.
* Rice and Peas: A hearty staple that helps balance the heat from the Scotch bonnet peppers. [3, 8, 9, 10] 

## Pro Tips for the Perfect Batch

* The "Crunch" Factor: For the most authentic experience, fry the fish until it is very crispy. This allows it to stay firm even after it has been "soaked" in the pickling sauce for several hours.
* Let it Marinate: While you can eat it immediately, the flavors truly shine if you let the fish sit in the sauce for at least an hour—or even overnight in the fridge. This allows the vinegar and spices to deeply penetrate the meat.
* Control the Heat: Scotch bonnet peppers are quite hot. To reduce the "burn" without losing the flavor, you can remove the seeds before slicing, or simply pierce the whole pepper and let it simmer in the sauce before removing it. [9, 11, 12, 13, 14, 15] 

## Variations to Try

* Fish Choices: While whole Red Snapper is the gold standard, you can also use Kingfish, Tilapia, Parrotfish, or even fish fillets if you prefer a boneless option.
* Colorful Medley: Many modern versions include thinly sliced bell peppers (red, green, or yellow) in the sauce to add extra color and a slight sweetness to the pickled topping.
* Herb Infusion: Some family recipes add fresh sprigs of thyme or a bay leaf to the vinegar as it simmers to add an earthy, aromatic depth. [9, 16, 17, 18]
